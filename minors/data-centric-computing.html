Data-Centric Computing Minor

The Data-Centric Computing minor focuses on how information is organized, stored, and retrieved. Students work with spreadsheets, relational and non-relational databases, and scripting, with exposure to geographic information systems (GIS). These skills are useful in fields that depend on managing large or complex collections of data, from scientific research to business and public health.

Why This Minor?

Students learn to represent information in tables and other data models, write queries to find what they need, and prepare data for further use. The emphasis is on working with the data itself: understanding its structure and keeping it usable.

This distinguishes the minor from Data Science. Data-Centric Computing concentrates on storing, organizing, and retrieving information; Data Science places more emphasis on statistics and interpreting results.

For students outside computer science, the minor provides practical tools for organizing, querying, and maintaining data in their own fields of study. These skills can make it easier to work independently with research data, records, and other collections of information, and to collaborate on data-related projects.

For computer science majors, the minor offers deeper practice designing databases and building software that stores, retrieves, and processes information. It also documents a particular interest and focus in data management within computer science.

Interdisciplinary Connections

The examples below highlight a few ways this minor could complement majors and programs elsewhere at Ithaca College. These are illustrative pairings, not an exhaustive list of possible connections.

Students in Environmental Science could organize geographic data to investigate changes in ecosystems. In Physics and Applied Physics, databases and visualizations can make experimental measurements easier to examine. Students in the Physics-Engineering 3-2 program might use the same techniques to manage engineering measurements.

In the School of Business, Business Analytics students could use database skills to prepare information for analysis. Accounting students might query financial records; Finance students could organize market data; and Marketing students might examine customer or campaign information. Sport Management students could work with attendance and event records.

For Journalism students, public records and datasets can be starting points for reporting. Students in Politics might examine election or policy data, while Sociology students could work with survey responses and population figures.

In Public and Community Health, organized population data can support investigations of health trends. Exercise Science students might work with training records or physiological measurements. In each field, the minor provides tools for handling information; the major provides the questions worth asking.

Course Descriptions

Working with Structured Data

This course starts with an in-depth coverage of Google Sheets followed by a brief introduction to relational databases. Daily and weekly projects are based on real-world datasets. COMP 11000 and HLTH 13901 are mutually exclusive; students may receive credit for only one.

Scripting in Spreadsheets

Introduces scripting in the context of Google Sheets. Exposes students to basic programming concepts so that students can make informed decisions about continuing with formal programming classes.

Working with Relational Data

This course introduces students to SQLite through hands-on experience working with existing databases. Emphasis is placed on querying data, creating views, and using SQLite tools to explore and manipulate relational data in a lightweight, standalone environment.

Foundations of CS I

This course is an introduction to solving problems by writing code. Course content includes the design, development, testing, and analyzing of python code. Also included are basic coding structures that are present across many programing languages, such as statements, loops, and functions.

Foundations of CS II

This course reinforces and extends the skills learned in "Foundations of CS I". It introduces object-oriented programming, functional programming, and basic algorithmic analysis. End-of-semester projects make use of APIs and libraries to access real-world data-sets and explore how programs can be useful to other disciplines or areas of interest.

Discrete Structures

This course covers the mathematical topics needed by Computer Science students. Topics include logic, sets, functions, relations, combinatorics, and discrete probability. Experimental work is conducted with Google Sheets.

Relational Database Systems

This course offers a comprehensive introduction to relational databases, emphasizing database design, query formulation, and optimization techniques. Students will learn to create and normalize database schemas, write advanced SQL queries, and improve performance through indexing and query optimization strategies. The course also introduces spatial data processing, including storage, querying, and analysis of geographic information within relational databases. Hands-on labs and projects reinforce practical skills using industry-standard tools.

Database Systems

Explores contemporary database topics such as noSQL databases, visualization, data-warehousing, and cloud-based infra-structures, extending the ideas learned in earlier database system coursework.
