Data-Centric Computing Minor

Our Data-Centric Computing minor gives students a practical foundation in working with data across a variety of formats and systems. Students learn how to generate, organize, store, and process data using tools such as spreadsheets, relational databases, non-relational databases, and scripting. Designed to complement fields such as the social and health sciences, accounting, business analytics, and finance, the minor helps students develop the technical skills needed to manage data effectively. Compared with the Data Science minor that focuses more heavily on statistics, our Data-Centric Computing minor emphasizes the representation, structure, storage, and querying of data, with additional exposure to Geographic Information Systems (GIS).

Why This Minor?

Students learn to move among useful ways of representing data, from spreadsheets to database tables and other data models. They practice querying, transforming, and managing information, with exposure to geographic information systems (GIS).

These two programs approach data from different directions. Data-Centric Computing emphasizes the structures and systems used to store and retrieve it; Data Science places greater emphasis on statistical analysis and drawing justified conclusions from the data.

For a computer science major, the Data-Centric Computing minor provides a focused pathway into the organization, management, and use of data. These skills underpin established work involving databases, data engineering, information systems, and data-intensive applications.

For a student in another major, it is a practical complement to subject-matter expertise. Students who know the questions their discipline asks can gain the tools to work directly with the information needed to investigate those questions.

Interdisciplinary Connections

Data plays a role in many fields, but the questions people ask about it are different. The Data-Centric Computing minor helps students collect, organize, search, and make sense of information from their own area of interest.

In the sciences, Environmental Science students might use geographic data to study changes in ecosystems, while students in Physics and Applied Physics could organize measurements from experiments and visualize their results. Students in the Physics-Engineering 3-2 program could use similar skills to work with engineering measurements and design data.

In business, Business Analytics has a particularly close connection: useful analysis begins with information that has been properly organized and prepared. Students in Accounting could learn to work with financial records, those in Finance with market data, and those in Marketing with customer and campaign information. Sport Management students might study attendance, events, or organizational data to support planning.

The minor can complement work in society and communication as well. Journalism students might investigate a story using public records and datasets. Politics students could explore election or public-policy data, while Sociology students might organize and examine survey responses or population statistics.

In health-related fields, Public and Community Health students could use community health records and population data to explore health trends. Exercise Science students might work with training and physiological measurements to better understand performance. In each case, the other major provides the subject knowledge, while Data-Centric Computing adds practical ways to work with the evidence.

One Possible Combination

A sociology, political science, psychology, or other social science major could combine that field with Data-Centric Computing to organize survey results, examine public datasets, and manage information for research or policy analysis. The disciplinary context supplies the questions; the minor strengthens the means of investigating them.

Course Descriptions

Working with Structured Data

This course starts with an in-depth coverage of Google Sheets followed by a brief introduction to relational databases. Daily and weekly projects are based on real-world datasets. COMP 11000 and HLTH 13901 are mutually exclusive; students may receive credit for only one.

Scripting in Spreadsheets

Introduces scripting in the context of Google Sheets. Exposes students to basic programming concepts so that students can make informed decisions about continuing with formal programming classes.

Working with Relational Data

This course introduces students to SQLite through hands-on experience working with existing databases. Emphasis is placed on querying data, creating views, and using SQLite tools to explore and manipulate relational data in a lightweight, standalone environment.

Computer Programming I

This course is an introduction to solving problems by writing code. Course content includes the design, development, testing, and analyzing of python code. Also included are basic coding structures that are present across many programing languages, such as statements, loops, and functions.

Computer Programming II

This course reinforces and extends the skills learned in "Computer Programming I". It introduces object-oriented programming, functional programming, and basic algorithmic analysis. End-of-semester projects make use of APIs and libraries to access real-world data-sets and explore how programs can be useful to other disciplines or areas of interest.

Discrete Structures

This course covers the mathematical topics needed by Computer Science students. Topics include logic, sets, functions, relations, combinatorics, and discrete probability. Experimental work is conducted with Google Sheets.

Relational Database Systems

This course offers a comprehensive introduction to relational databases, emphasizing database design, query formulation, and optimization techniques. Students will learn to create and normalize database schemas, write advanced SQL queries, and improve performance through indexing and query optimization strategies. The course also introduces spatial data processing, including storage, querying, and analysis of geographic information within relational databases. Hands-on labs and projects reinforce practical skills using industry-standard tools.

Database Systems

Explores contemporary database topics such as noSQL databases, visualization, data-warehousing, and cloud-based infra-structures, extending the ideas learned in earlier database system coursework.
