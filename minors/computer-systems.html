Computer Systems Minor

The Computer Systems minor examines what happens beneath the software we use: how computers run programs, how operating systems manage their work, and how devices communicate over networks. The minor is particularly relevant to students interested in operating systems, network infrastructure, or cybersecurity.

Why This Minor?

Students study the organization of computers, the execution of programs, and the operating systems that coordinate them. They learn how information moves through networks and what happens when a computer runs several processes at once.

In practical work, students connect machines and configure communication between them, building a small-scale network rather than treating the Internet as a black box. The same concepts are important when investigating network problems, software performance, and security.

For students outside computer science, the minor provides a practical understanding of the computers, instruments, and connected devices used in their fields. It can help them make informed technology choices, troubleshoot problems, and communicate with technical specialists.

For computer science majors, the minor offers deeper study and hands-on experience with operating systems, networking, and the interaction between hardware and software. It also documents a particular interest and focus within computer science, including areas related to infrastructure and cybersecurity.

Interdisciplinary Connections

The examples below highlight a few ways this minor could complement majors and programs elsewhere at Ithaca College. These are illustrative pairings, not an exhaustive list of possible connections.

In Applied Physics, scientific instruments often combine sensors, computers, and software. Systems knowledge can help students understand how these components communicate. Physics students may find the same knowledge useful when an experiment depends on computing hardware, operating systems, or processing performance.

Students in the Physics-Engineering 3-2 program could apply an understanding of hardware and software interactions to computer-based engineering systems.

Sound Recording Technology offers a connection outside the sciences. Digital recording involves audio interfaces, computers, operating systems, and software whose timing matters. Knowing how those components work together can be valuable when setting up or troubleshooting a recording system.

Course Descriptions

Foundations of CS I

This course is an introduction to solving problems by writing code. Course content includes the design, development, testing, and analyzing of python code. Also included are basic coding structures that are present across many programing languages, such as statements, loops, and functions.

Foundations of CS II

This course reinforces and extends the skills learned in "Foundations of CS I". It introduces object-oriented programming, functional programming, and basic algorithmic analysis. End-of-semester projects make use of APIs and libraries to access real-world data-sets and explore how programs can be useful to other disciplines or areas of interest.

Computer Systems I

This course explores how computers work at the lowest level. It starts with the language C to understand how programs can talk to the operating system (e.g., Windows, Mac OS, Linux) and then looks at how programs are actually run on a computer's processor. This course demystifies the operation of the modern computer.

Discrete Structures

This course covers the mathematical topics needed by Computer Science students. Topics include logic, sets, functions, relations, combinatorics, and discrete probability. Experimental work is conducted with Google Sheets.

Computer Systems II

This course is a continuation of "Computer Systems I" providing in-depth investigations of the major concepts, algorithms, and implementation principles of computer systems. In particular, computers of all types (including networks and mobile devices) run many apps at the same time, called "concurrent programming". It is very challenging to write apps that run concurrently, and this course explores techniques to do this on computers and networks. Concepts include concurrent apps (processes), threads, networked apps and parallel processes.

Computer Networks

This course covers the concepts of computer networks and data communications. The major topics include application layer protocols, transport layer protocols, routing, software defined networks, local area networks (including wifi), network security. The course has numerous lab components based on network-programming and packet tracing.
